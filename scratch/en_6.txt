The Advaitin themselves (to strengthen their own doctrine) examines various definitions of 'Mithyatva' (falsity). In Advaita texts (such as Advaita Siddhi), this is the **'Svapaksha-Vimarsha'** (self-examination). The Advaitin attempts to present a refined definition of falsity; in this process, they first present possible definitions and then demonstrate the flaws in them themselves.

**1. Nyayamrutam: Mithyatvam is not absolute non-existence (atyantasattvam), due to contradiction of doctrine (apasiddhanta).**
This presents a crucial argument in Advaita Vedanta. The 'Mithyatva' accepted in Advaita is not 'absolute non-existence' (atyantasattvam). If we consider falsity to be absolute non-existence, then it would lead to the destruction of our own doctrine, which is called 'Apasiddhanta' (contradiction of doctrine).

**What is 'absolute non-existence'?**
In the world, those things are called 'absolutely non-existent' which are never perceived anywhere, at any time, by any means of knowledge. For example, a **'hare's horn'** or the **'son of a barren woman'**. These things exist merely as words; their appearance never happens.

**Why is falsity not absolute non-existence?**
In the Advaita doctrine, 'Mithya' (false) is said to be that which is perceived directly but is later sublated by knowledge. For example, the **'rope-snake'**. In the dark, the knowledge of a snake occurs on a rope. That snake appears there, and it even causes fear. Therefore, it is not completely non-existent like a 'hare's horn'. If that snake were absolutely non-existent, it would never have been perceived.
Similarly, this **world** is false, but it is not absolutely non-existent. Because we all perceive the world, interact in it, and experience pleasure and pain. Thus, the falsity of the world is not 'voidness' (shunyatva).

**The implication of 'Apasiddhanta':**
Here, the word 'Apasiddhanta' is very important. In Advaita Vedanta, three levels of reality are accepted:
1. Paramarthika Satta (Absolute Reality - Brahman)
2. **Vyavaharika Satta (Empirical Reality - World)**
3. Pratibhasika Satta (Apparent Reality - Dream, rope-snake, etc.)

If we accept the meaning of falsity as 'absolute non-existence', then:
* **The empirical reality would be destroyed:** In the Advaita doctrine, the world is considered true from an 'empirical perspective'. If it were absolutely non-existent, how would all these interactions (eating, walking, studying) be possible?
* **Objection of Sunyavada (Nihilism):** If everything is false meaning absolutely non-existent (void), then the Advaita doctrine would become identical to Buddhist Sunyavada. But Vedanta is not nihilistic.
* **Contradiction with valid knowledge (Pramana):** Our eyes and other senses perceive the world. If the world were absolutely non-existent, it would contradict direct perception itself.

**Conclusion:**
Thus, it is clear that the meaning of falsity should never be taken as 'absolute non-existence'. Falsity is something that appears (is capable of appearing), but is not true in all three periods of time. Therefore, to protect one's own doctrine (to avoid Apasiddhanta), falsity must be considered different from absolute non-existence.

**2. Nyayamrutam: It is not indefinability (anirvacyatvam), as it is unestablished (aprasiddhi).**
Falsity cannot be said to be 'indefinability' (Anirvacyatvam), because such a thing is **'unestablished'** (aprasiddham) in the world.

**What is Anirvacyatvam?**
Advaita Vedantins say that the world is 'indefinable'. Indefinable means - that which cannot be called 'real' (sat) (because it gets sublated), and cannot be called 'unreal' (asat) (because it is perceived). Such a peculiar nature is called 'Anirvacyam'.

**The logic behind 'Aprasiddheḥ':**
When a definition of something is given, that definition must be well-known in the world. The objection here is that a thing called 'Anirvacyatvam' is not known in the world at all.
* **Contradiction with worldly experience:** Only two categories are known in the world - **'Sat'** (Real, like the self) or **'Asat'** (Unreal, like a hare's horn). A third category which is different from both 'real and unreal' does not exist in our experience.
* **Lack of valid knowledge (Pramana):** By what means of knowledge is 'Anirvacyatvam' established? Direct perception only reveals the existence or absence of a thing. A peculiar thing called 'indefinable' is not seen by perception.
* **Avyapti / Asiddhi (Unestablished):** If indefinability is not established anywhere, how can it be used as a definition? That which itself is not established cannot serve as a definition for something else (falsity).

**Deep implication:**
The question here is - "Is there any such thing which is neither real nor unreal?"
If they say the 'rope-snake' is indefinable, then the logician asks - even the rope-snake becomes 'unreal' (asat) upon attaining true knowledge. So it falls into the category of 'unreal', not into any third category. Thus, **'Anirvacyatvam'** is merely a technical term; it is unestablished in the world.

**Conclusion:**
Therefore, the implication is that the use of the term 'Anirvacyatvam' in discussing falsity is logically difficult, because in logic or in ordinary discourse, there are only two paths: 'real' and 'unreal'. The third 'indefinable' path is unknown in the world.

**3. Nyayamrutam: It is not 'difference from the real' (sadviviktatvam), because even a real object is different from another real object.**
This refutes the third possible definition of falsity. The logical flaw in defining it as 'difference from the real' (sadviviktatvam) is discussed here.

**Proposed Definition:**
Someone might say - **"Falsity is difference from the real"**.
This means - whatever is different from the 'real' (sat) is called 'false'. For example - Brahman is real, so whatever is different from it (the world, etc.) is false.

**The Reason for Rejection:**
The Advaita Vedantins say - **"because even a real object is different from another real object" (sato'pi sadantaraviviktatvāt)**. The argument is as follows:
* In the world, many things are considered 'real' (from an empirical viewpoint). For example - a 'pot' is real, a 'cloth' is also real.
* But a 'pot' is different from a 'cloth'. That is, a pot is 'distinct' from a cloth.
* Here, the pot is also 'real', and the cloth is also 'real'.
* If we say that **'falsity is difference from the real'**, then since the pot is different from the cloth, the pot would become 'false'.
* Similarly, since the cloth is different from the pot (which is real), the cloth would also become 'false'.

When Advaita Vedantins give the definition 'falsity is difference from the real', the flaw of **'Siddha-sadhana'** occurs.

**Meaning of Siddha-sadhana:**
"Proving what is already established is Siddha-sadhana." In an argument, if something is already accepted by the opponent, proving it again is called 'Siddha-sadhana' (proving the proven). This is considered a flaw in logic because it doesn't prove anything new.

**Context of Siddha-sadhana here:**
When the Advaitin says - **"The world is false, because it is different from the real"**.
The Advaitin's goal is to prove the 'falsity' of the world. But here the reason 'difference from the real' is flawed:
* **Opponent's (Dualist/Logician's) view:** Logicians believe there are many objects in the world, and they are all 'real'. Like - a 'pot' is real, a 'cloth' is real.
* **Acceptance of difference:** The logician already accepts that **a pot is different from a cloth.** Meaning, a pot is distinct from the 'real' cloth.
* **Arising of the flaw:**
  1. Advaitin wants to prove - **"The world is false"**.
  2. Logician says - "I already accept that a 'pot' is different from another 'real' thing (a cloth). This 'difference from the real' is already established in my doctrine too."
  3. Thus, the reason given by the Advaitin is already accepted by the logician regarding pots and cloths. But according to the logician, this difference does not prove 'falsity', rather it proves 'difference between objects'.

**Final Analysis:**
If 'difference from the real' were the definition of falsity, then:
* **Advaitin says:** A pot is different from the 'real' (cloth), so the pot is false.
* **Logician says:** A pot is different from the 'real' (cloth), this is **already established** by me. In my view, this difference is 'real'. You are only proving what I already accept.
Thus, **'Siddha-sadhana'** occurs. The Advaitin cannot prove any new 'falsity'; they only prove the mutual 'difference' of objects, which is already established according to the Dualists.

**4. Nyayamrutam: It is not 'the absence of the attribute of existence' (sattvanadhikaranatvam), because this would over-apply to the attribute-less Brahman. Nor can it be said that precisely because Brahman is attribute-less, the absence of existence is also not there; because the reason 'being attribute-less' and the probandum 'the absence of the absence of existence' would contradict each other as presence and absence, making it impossible to deny a negative attribute. Otherwise, 'the absence of non-existence' would also be absent there, and to exclude that, the qualification 'the absence of existence' could not be used in the definition of indefinability.**

The Advaitin wants to prove that the world is 'false'. For this, they propose a definition - **"Falsity is not being the locus of existence"**. Meaning, there is no 'existence' (sattva) in the world, so the world is false.
They see that if they define falsity as the **'absence of the attribute of existence'**, then their definition will over-apply to **Brahman** itself. How?
* **Brahman's attribute-lessness:** In the Advaita doctrine, Brahman is 'attribute-less' (nirdharmaka). It has no attributes.
* **Existence is an attribute:** 'Existence-ness' (sattvam) is an attribute.
* **Absence of existence in Brahman:** Since Brahman is attribute-less, the attribute called 'existence' does not reside in Brahman. (Note: Brahman *is* Existence, but it does not *have* the property of existence).
* **Over-application:** Thus, Brahman also became 'not the locus of existence' (devoid of the attribute of existence). If this were the definition of falsity, then Brahman would also be called 'false'.

If the Advaitin declares their own Brahman as 'false', their entire doctrine would be destroyed.
* **Target:** The world is false.
* **Fear:** The danger of falsity applying to Brahman too.
Therefore, the Advaitin refutes this definition - **"because even the attribute-less Brahman possesses it"** (so this definition is unacceptable).

**'Sattvam' and Brahman (Technical Distinction):**
Here, the Advaitin makes a subtle distinction:
1. **Brahman = Pure Existence:** Brahman is existence itself; existence does not reside there as an attribute.
2. **Sattvam = Attribute of being real:** This attribute can be established in the world, but not in Brahman.

**Conclusion:**
The Advaitin concludes that - **'absence of the attribute of existence'** cannot be falsity. Because doing so would lead to the over-application of falsity to the attribute-less Brahman, which contradicts our doctrine.

**|| Dialogue on the determination of falsity ||**

**Disciple:** O Lord! In the Advaita doctrine, the world is said to be 'false'. What is the definition of this falsity? Is falsity 'absolute non-existence' (like a hare's horn)?
**Guru:** No, child! **"Falsity is not absolute non-existence, due to contradiction of doctrine."**
If the world were absolutely non-existent, it would not even appear. But we see the world and interact in it. If we accept absolute non-existence, it will contradict perception, which would be our **'Apasiddhanta'**.

**Disciple:** Then is falsity **'indefinability'**?
**Guru:** **"Nor is it indefinability, as it is unestablished."**
In the world, only 'real' and 'unreal' are known. A thing distinct from both is unknown. Thus, being unestablished, this definition cannot be given initially.

**Disciple:** Okay. Then let's say - **'Falsity is being different from the real'**. Whatever is different from the truth is false.
**Guru:** **"Nor is it difference from the real, because even a real object is different from another real object."**
This leads to the flaw of 'Siddha-sadhana'. A pot is real, and a cloth is real. A pot is different from a cloth. If 'being different from the real' meant falsity, then the pot would be 'false' because it is different from a cloth. Thus all real objects would be called false. Dualists consider difference to be real, so this difference is already established for them, and falsity is not proven.

**Disciple:** Then let's look at the fourth definition - **'Falsity is the absence of the attribute of existence'**. That which lacks the attribute of existence is false.
**Guru:** Child! Here over-application occurs - **"Because even the attribute-less Brahman has it."**
Brahman is attribute-less, so it lacks the attribute of existence. Thus Brahman also becomes 'not the locus of existence'. If this is falsity, Brahman would become false!

**Disciple:** Lord! You pointed out the over-application in Brahman for this definition. Since Brahman is attribute-less, it lacks the attribute of existence.
But I have a doubt - Brahman is **'attribute-less'**. 'Absence of existence' is an 'absence'. Absence itself is also an 'attribute'. If Brahman has no attributes, then the attribute of **'absence of existence'** will also not exist in it. Thus, the over-application in Brahman naturally disappears, doesn't it?

**Guru (Acharya):** Child! **"Nor can it be said..."** Your argument is illogical.
**Disciple:** Why, Acharya?
**Guru:** Listen. The logical flaw of **'Contradiction' (Vyaghata)** occurs here. You say Brahman is 'attribute-less', so it lacks 'absence of existence'. According to the rule of logic - **'the negation of an absence is a presence'**.
If Brahman lacks 'absence of existence', it means Brahman **'has the attribute of existence'**. If Brahman has the attribute of existence, how can your claim of **'attribute-lessness'** be established? Saying an attribute exists in the attribute-less Brahman is self-contradictory, right? This is the **"contradiction between presence and absence"**.

**Disciple:** If 'negative attributes' are accepted in Brahman, won't it destroy Advaita (by destroying Brahman's attribute-lessness)?
**Guru:** Advaita Vedantins solve this using the doctrine of **'absence as the very nature' (Svarupatmaka-abhava)**.
**Absence is not a separate entity**
In Advaita doctrine, **'absence is the very nature of its locus'**.
* When we say "there is no pot on the floor", logically that absence is **'just the floor'**.
* Similarly, when we say "Brahman has the absence of existence", that absence is not an 'attribute', but **Brahman's very nature**. Brahman itself is of the nature of the absence of attributes. Thus, by not accepting a separate attribute, the doctrine of 'attribute-lessness' is not harmed.

**Negation of Imagined Attributes**
Advaitins say that to prove Brahman's attribute-lessness, the 'absences' we use (like non-duality, quality-lessness) are spoken from an **'empirical perspective'**.
* When we say 'Advaitam' (absence of duality), the goal is to negate duality.
* Once duality is negated, that 'absence' also dissolves into Brahman's nature.
* Just as fire burns wood and then extinguishes itself, negative attributes negate the universe and merge into Brahman's nature.

**Re-addressing the Contradiction**
Your question was - "if negative attributes exist, Advaita is harmed". But the logic goes like this:
* If we negate **'negative attributes'** too, then we would inevitably have to accept **'positive attributes'**.
* If we say 'absence of existence' does not exist in Brahman, then the 'positive attribute of existence' would be established in Brahman.
* A positive attribute is more harmful to Advaita doctrine than a negative one. Because accepting a positive attribute makes Brahman 'saguna' (possessing qualities), destroying Advaita's main doctrine.

Thus, Advaita doctrine arranges it like this:
1. **From absolute reality:** Brahman has neither positive nor negative attributes. It is devoid of the universe.
2. **From logical discourse:** To protect Brahman's reality, the absence of 'positive attributes' must be accepted. That absence is Brahman's nature, not a separate attribute.
Therefore it was said - **"Because even the attribute-less Brahman has it"**. Meaning - due to the absence of positive attributes in Brahman, over-application definitely occurs.

**Disciple:** True, Acharya! I hadn't thought of that. The negation of an absence brings presence, destroying attribute-lessness. So 'absence of existence' must be accepted in Brahman. But Acharya, why is the next line **"Otherwise, the absence of non-existence would also..."** spoken?
**Guru:** Its implication is very deep. In Advaita philosophy, the definition of falsity is given as - **"Absence of non-existence while being the absence of existence"**.
There are two terms in this definition:
1. **Absence of non-existence:** (Should not be 'unreal' like a hare's horn). This is to exclude 'unreal' objects.
2. **Absence of existence:** (Should not be 'real' like Brahman). This is to exclude 'Brahman'.
If your logic were true that "nothing exists in Brahman due to its attribute-lessness", then Brahman would also not have **'absence of non-existence'**. If it doesn't have that, Brahman is automatically excluded from this definition.
Then why did the Acharya provide the qualification **'absence of existence'** in the definition? Its purpose is precisely to exclude Brahman. If Brahman didn't have the absence of existence, this qualification would be redundant. But the Acharyas provided it, proving that **'absence of existence'** logically resides in Brahman.

**Disciple:** Wow! It's clear now.
1. Brahman is attribute-less, so it lacks the positive attribute of existence.
2. Therefore Brahman inevitably has the **'absence of existence'**.
3. Therefore the definition 'falsity is the absence of existence' **over-applies to Brahman**.
For this reason, this fourth view is also flawed.

**Guru:** Well done, child! You understood correctly.

**5. Nyayamrutam: It is not 'being un-objectifiable by valid knowledge' (pramityavishayatvam), because Brahman is pervaded by the mental modification generated by Vedanta. And even shell-silver is an object through primary cognition and for secondary cognition, and directly as the object of negation for sublating knowledge. If it means not being directly the object of valid knowledge where reality is the chief qualifier, then due to parsimony, falsity would simply be the absence of existence.**

**Disciple:** Guru! How do Advaitins examine their own definitions of falsity? Now it's the fifth definition's turn. What do they say?
**Guru:** Child! Look, to strengthen their view, Advaitins first propose a simple definition - **"Falsity is being un-objectifiable by valid knowledge"**.
Their intent is - whatever is not the object of 'Prama' (valid knowledge) is 'false'. Like the rope-snake.
**Disciple:** But Guru! In Advaita doctrine, Brahman is also 'beyond speech and mind' (un-objectifiable). Then how do they not see the flaw of **'over-application'** in this definition themselves?
**Guru:** They do see it! That's why they themselves argue - **"It is not being un-objectifiable by valid knowledge, because Brahman is pervaded by the mental modification generated by Vedanta."**
To protect their view, they deliberate thus:
* "Although Brahman is called 'un-objectifiable', it is not completely unknown. When a seeker hears Vedanta sentences, an 'unbroken mental modification' (Akhandakara-vritti) arises in their intellect. That modification pervades Brahman. So Brahman is **'pervaded by the mental modification generated by Vedanta'**. Thus Brahman is not 'unknowable'. So there will be no over-application in Brahman."

**Disciple:** Wow! Their argument is very clever. They prove Brahman's objectifiability through 'vritti-vyapyatva'. Then this definition became flawless according to them, right?
**Guru:** No, child! Right here is the internal contradiction in Advaita doctrine, which they themselves accept. In Advaita, 'knowledge' is twofold:
1. **Vritti-vyapyatvam (Pervasion by mental modification):** Merely breaking the veil covering Brahman by the modification.
2. **Phala-vyapyatvam (Pervasion by result):** Where the light of knowledge (consciousness-result) falls on the object.
**The Advaitins' own conclusion is this:**
Brahman is 'pervaded by modification' (to break the veil), but Brahman is **'not pervaded by the result'** (because Brahman is self-luminous, it doesn't need another light to illuminate it).
**Disciple:** Then what is the consequence for this 'definition'?
**Guru:** According to Advaitins, 'Pramiti' (Valid Knowledge) is said only when the object is **'pervaded by the result'**. Since Brahman is **'not pervaded by the result'**, logically Brahman remains **'not the object of valid knowledge'**.
So if they call 'being un-objectifiable by valid knowledge' falsity, then **Brahman also becomes false**.
**Disciple:** Wow! Now I understand their method of deliberation.
1. They first define 'being un-objectifiable by valid knowledge'.
2. Then they try to protect Brahman using 'pervasion by modification'.
3. But ultimately their protective shield breaks by their own doctrine of 'non-pervasion by result' (self-luminosity).
4. So **'over-application'** in Brahman is unavoidable.
**Guru:** True. That's why Advaitins themselves say - "Nor can it be said...". Meaning this argument also fails.

**Disciple:** Guru! Advaitins examine the definition 'falsity is being un-objectifiable by valid knowledge'. Earlier they protected Brahman. Now how do they see this definition applying to **shell-silver** (a false object)?
**Guru:** True, child! Their deliberation is very objective and logical. They see that if 'un-objectifiability' meant falsity, then shell-silver shouldn't be the object of knowledge. But in the world, it does become an object.
**Disciple:** How does it become an object? It's false, right?
**Guru:** Look through their logic - **"And even shell-silver is an object through primary cognition and for secondary cognition, and directly as the object of negation for sublating knowledge."**
Three reasons are given here:
1. **Primary & Secondary Knowledge:**
When someone mistakenly sees silver in a shell, they know - "This is silver" (This is **primary cognition**). Then they think - "I know silver" (This is **secondary cognition**). Here 'silver' became the object of knowledge. So silver didn't remain 'un-objectifiable'.
2. **Objectivity towards sublating knowledge:**
When in the light, true knowledge occurs - **"This is not silver"**, then too silver becomes the object of knowledge. How? **'As an object of negation'**. What is negated? The silver itself. So in the negating knowledge, silver is indeed the object.
3. **Direct Objectivity:**
When we say "silver doesn't exist", 'silver' stands directly in our intellect as an object, otherwise its negation is impossible.

**Disciple:** Wow! Then what is the Advaitins' conclusion?
**Guru:** The result of their deliberation is just this - "Even a false object (silver) somehow becomes the object of knowledge. So the simple definition **'being un-objectifiable by valid knowledge'** is afflicted by the flaw of 'under-application' (Avyapti), because it doesn't apply to false objects like silver."

**Disciple:** Now I understand clearly. Why Advaitins say "Nor can it be said..." and refine this definition. They know that what is negated (silver) also becomes an object of knowledge. So 'absence of objectifiability' cannot be falsity.

**Disciple:** Guru! Earlier it was proven that shell-silver (false object) becomes the object of knowledge. Then what new refinement do Advaitins make to protect their definition?
**Guru:** Child! They are very intelligent. They themselves refine it thus - **"If it means not being directly the object of valid knowledge where reality is the chief qualifier..."**.
Their thought is - "Let it not be merely 'objectifiability'. We say - whatever does not become the object of valid knowledge **'as real'**, is false."
Shell-silver is an object of knowledge, but it is not known 'as real' by any valid knowledge. So silver is proven false.
**Disciple:** Wow! This refinement seems proper. With this, the 'under-application' in shell-silver is gone, and the 'over-application' in Brahman (which is the object of real knowledge) will not occur either.
**Guru:** Look child! Right here comes the **deliberation on parsimony and complexity (Laghu-Gaurava)**. Advaitins themselves think - **"Due to parsimony, falsity would simply be the absence of existence."**
**Disciple:** What does this mean?
**Guru:** The simple meaning is this - "If you say that 'this doesn't become the object of true knowledge', then what is its direct meaning? Its meaning is - **there is no 'reality' (Sattva) in that object.**"
Look at the logic path of the Advaitins:
1. Saying "not being the object of true knowledge" is 'complex' (heavy).
2. Saying "absence of reality" is 'parsimonious' (simple/direct).
The rule in logic is that where parsimony works, complexity is not accepted.

**Disciple:** Then the Advaitins came back to exactly where they were in the fourth definition?
**Guru:** True! **'Absence of existence'** was indeed the fourth definition. Why was that definition abandoned by them earlier? Because there was **over-application in Brahman** (because even the attribute-less Brahman has it).
So the conclusion of the Advaitins' deliberation is this -
* If they try to protect the fifth definition, it dissolves back into the fourth definition (absence of existence).
* The fourth definition is already proven flawed.
* Therefore, the definition "Falsity is being un-objectifiable by valid knowledge" also proves to be unfit in deliberation.

**Disciple:** Wow! The analytical skill of the Advaitins is amazing. They themselves see how a new definition ends up in an old flaw. So they abandon the fifth definition too and move forward.
**Guru:** Well done, child! The point is - if 'not being the object of knowledge as real' is called falsity, then by parsimony its meaning becomes 'absence of existence'. The fourth definition 'absence of existence' is already refuted due to over-application in Brahman. So the fifth definition is also proven unrefined due to lack of logical parsimony. Thus, by examining all views in order, they will state the final doctrine at the end.

**Disciple:** Guru! The deliberation on the fifth definition was very long. Please tell me the **summary** of all this briefly.
**Guru:** Certainly child! Understand this deliberation in five steps:

**1. Nature of the definition**
The fifth possible view of the Advaitins is - **"Falsity is being un-objectifiable by valid knowledge"**. Meaning whatever doesn't become the object of knowledge is false.

**Guru:** But finally the conclusion of their deliberation is this - that saying 'not being the object of true knowledge' is very complex. By parsimony its meaning is - **"Absence of Reality"**.
